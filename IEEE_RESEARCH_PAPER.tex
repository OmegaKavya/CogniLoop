\begin{abstract}
Passive video consumption dominates modern online education platforms (such as MOOCs), failing to guarantee conceptual mastery. Traditional Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS) offer structured adaptation but suffer from severe content-authoring bottlenecks, making open-domain scaling cost-prohibitive. This paper presents \textit{CogniLoop}, a hybrid adaptive learning framework that converts passive video viewing into a closed-loop mastery system. The system integrates: (1) an offline-capable Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline for transcript-grounded, low-cost question generation; (2) an Item Response Theory (IRT)-grounded Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT) engine for dynamic cognitive state estimation; (3) a Thompson Sampling Contextual Bandit policy for optimal difficulty selection; (4) a $K$-Means clustering classifier to model student learning pacing; and (5) a timing-attack resistant PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 password hashing module for secure user authentication. Evaluation under a two-stage framework—comprising algorithmic simulation profiles ($N=60$) and statistical effect size analysis—demonstrates that our adaptive framework yields a statistically significant increase in Normalized Learning Gain (NLG) compared to static sequential instruction ($p < 0.0001$, Cohen's $d = 1.088$). We discuss the system's design, mathematical formulations, ablation analysis, limitations, and engineering strategies implemented to ensure fault tolerance, factual grounding, and low-latency execution.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{O}{nline} video education has democratized access to high-quality learning materials. Platforms like YouTube and open-domain university lecture repositories host thousands of hours of computer science lectures. However, video lectures remain a passive learning medium. Research in cognitive psychology shows that passive watching leads to poor retention, rapid cognitive decay, and a false sense of competence. 

To bridge this gap, active learning interventions (such as embedded in-video checkpoints, post-video quizzes, and personalized review paths) are required. While traditional Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS) like ASSISTments or Cognitive Tutors effectively implement these loops, they are constrained by the \textbf{Content Authoring Bottleneck}. Authoring high-quality conceptual questions, distractor options representing specific misconceptions, hints, and explanations requires hundreds of hours of manual labor by domain experts. Consequently, traditional ITS cannot scale to open-domain video repositories.

Conversely, while Large Language Models (LLMs) can generate assessments instantly, they suffer from two critical limitations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Hallucination and Lack of Context}: Unconstrained LLMs generate generic, out-of-context questions that do not align with the specific content covered in a video.
    \item \textbf{Pedagogical Blindness}: Standard LLM chat interfaces function as conversational partners but lack structured cognitive modeling. They cannot track student knowledge states over time or mathematically optimize the learning path.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Our Contributions}
We propose \textit{CogniLoop}, a production-ready, open-domain adaptive learning platform that addresses these gaps. Our contribution is twofold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Integrated Adaptive Architecture}: We present an open-domain adaptive tutoring framework that combines local RAG-based vector retriever indexing (\texttt{ChromaDB}), sentence embeddings (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}), IRT-parameterized BKT skill tracking, $K$-Means interaction behavior modeling, and a Thompson Sampling Contextual Bandit for difficulty routing. This architecture substantially minimizes hallucination risk by grounding LLM prompt generation in exact transcript context chunks.
    \item \textbf{Empirical and Statistical Validation}: We evaluate the platform under a two-stage validation framework: Phase 1 algorithmic simulation ($N=60$) demonstrating significant learning gain improvements ($\Delta \text{NLG} = +0.571, p < 0.0001, U = 682.5, d = 1.088$) alongside an ablation study isolating difficulty routing and retrieval grounding components, and Phase 2 human pilot protocols.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work and Comparative Analysis}
To establish the necessity of our hybrid framework, we contrast it against traditional ITS and commercial learning platforms.

\subsection{Traditional ITS vs. Commercial MOOCs and LMS}
Commercial Massive Open Online Course (MOOC) platforms like Coursera and Udemy have scaled online education, but personalization is restricted to coarse-grained course recommendations \cite{zhang2019hybrid}. Coursera utilizes collaborative filtering \cite{lan2016reinforcement}, while Udemy relies on ratings \cite{aher2012hybrid}. 

We implement Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT) following Corbett \& Anderson \cite{corbett1994knowledge} because classic cognitive modeling provides deterministic, interpretable concept mastery estimates from initial student attempts without requiring thousands of pre-training interaction sequences needed by deep learning architectures \cite{piech2015deep, pelanek2017bayesian}. Furthermore, we incorporate Item Response Theory (IRT) principles \cite{baker2008more, koedinger1997intelligent} and contextual reinforcement learning \cite{clement2015multi, shen2022contextual} to adjust question difficulty dynamically.

Enterprise Learning Management Systems (LMS) such as Oracle Fusion Cloud Learning implement AI-driven skill-gap analyses \cite{oracle2023ai}. However, they lack fine-grained cognitive state estimation during active video viewing sessions.

\subsection{In-Video Active Checkpoint Competitors}
Platforms like Edpuzzle allow instructors to insert active checkpoints. However, this relies on manual annotation and is static, violating the Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) \cite{bloom19842}.

\subsection{Item Response Theory (IRT) Grounded BKT}
BKT models student knowledge as a latent binary variable (learned vs. unlearned) \cite{corbett1994knowledge}. At each quiz attempt $n$, the system tracks the probability $P(L_n)$ that a student has mastered a specific concept. The update is parameterized by prior $P(L_0) = 0.3$, transition $P(T) = 0.2$, guess probability $P(G)$, and slip probability $P(S)$ \cite{baker2008more, lan2014time}.

We integrate IRT principles by dynamically mapping guess and slip values based on quiz difficulty:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Easy}: $P(G) = 0.30, P(S) = 0.05$
    \item \textbf{Medium}: $P(G) = 0.20, P(S) = 0.10$
    \item \textbf{Hard}: $P(G) = 0.10, P(S) = 0.15$
\end{itemize}

Upon observing response $Obs \in \{\text{Correct}, \text{Incorrect}\}$, the posterior probability is:
\begin{equation}
P(L_n \mid \text{Correct}) = \frac{P(L_n)(1 - P(S))}{P(L_n)(1 - P(S)) + (1 - P(L_n))P(G)}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
P(L_n \mid \text{Incorrect}) = \frac{P(L_n)P(S)}{P(L_n)P(S) + (1 - P(L_n))(1 - P(G))}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
P(L_{n+1}) = P(L_n \mid Obs) + (1 - P(L_n \mid Obs))P(T)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Thompson Sampling Contextual Bandit}
We formulate difficulty selection as a contextual multi-armed bandit problem optimized using \textbf{Thompson Sampling} \cite{clement2015multi, shen2022contextual}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{State Space}: $s = \langle C, M_{bin} \rangle$ where $C \in \{\text{Steady}, \text{Detail}, \text{Fast}\}$ and $M_{bin} \in \{\text{Low}, \text{Medium}, \text{High}\}$.
    \item \textbf{Reward Function}: $R(s,a) = (\text{Score} / 100.0) \times w_a$ where $w_{\text{easy}}=0.8, w_{\text{medium}}=1.0, w_{\text{hard}}=1.2$.
    \item \textbf{Beta-Bernoulli Update}:
    \begin{equation}
    \alpha_{s,a} \leftarrow \alpha_{s,a} + r, \quad \beta_{s,a} \leftarrow \beta_{s,a} + (1-r)
    \end{equation}
    where $r = R / 1.2 \in [0, 1]$.
    \item \textbf{Action Selection}:
    \begin{equation}
    \theta_a \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_{s,a}, \beta_{s,a}), \quad a^* = \arg\max_{a \in A} (\theta_a \times w_a)
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Behavioral Micro-Pattern Clustering}
Student interaction dynamics during video playback correlate strongly with learning strategies and dropout behavior \cite{jovanovic2017learning, sinha2014your}. We implement a $K$-Means clustering model ($K=3$) using features:
\begin{equation}
x_i = \langle \text{pause\_count}, \text{rewatch\_count}, \text{skip\_ratio}, \text{watch\_percentage} \rangle
\end{equation}

\subsection{Engagement, Retention, and Improvement (ERI) Optimization}
CogniLoop integrates retrieval practice and active testing \cite{dunlosky2013improving, karpicke2008critical} with RAG retrieval grounding \cite{lewis2020retrieval, gao2023retrieval, lau2024evaluating, prasath2024retrieval}.

\section{Experimental Validation}
Our evaluation framework is organized into a two-stage structure: \textbf{Phase 1: Algorithmic Simulation Validation ($N=60$)} to establish mathematical convergence and effect sizes under controlled conditions, and \textbf{Phase 2: Human Pilot Protocol ($N=20$)} for live participant trials.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig2_results.png}
\caption{Normalized Learning Gain (NLG) across Learner Archetypes.}
\label{fig_results}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Control Group Mean NLG ($\overline{\text{NLG}}_{\text{ctrl}}$)}: $0.363 \pm 0.716$
    \item \textbf{Experimental Group Mean NLG ($\overline{\text{NLG}}_{\text{exp}}$)}: $0.934 \pm 0.196$
    \item \textbf{Gain Delta ($\Delta \text{NLG}$)}: $+0.571$ ($+157.3\%$ improvement over control)
    \item \textbf{Two-Sample $t$-Statistic}: $t = 4.215, p = 0.000088 < 0.0001$
    \item \textbf{Mann-Whitney $U$ Statistic}: $U = 682.5, p = 0.000506 < 0.001$ (Non-parametric significance)
    \item \textbf{Cohen's $d$ Effect Size}: $d = 1.088$ (Large effect magnitude, $d > 0.8$)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Component Ablation Analysis}
To isolate component contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full CogniLoop Pipeline}: Mean NLG $= 0.934$
    \item \textbf{Ablation Variant A (Fixed Difficulty - No Contextual Bandit)}: Mean NLG $= 0.512$ ($\Delta \text{NLG} = -0.422$), demonstrating that Thompson Sampling difficulty adjustment drives significant mastery optimization.
    \item \textbf{Ablation Variant B (Ungrounded Prompts - No RAG)}: Increases question context mismatch from $2.1\%$ to $28.4\%$, confirming that transcript embedding retrieval is essential for conceptual alignment.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion, Feasibility, and Limitations}

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Simulated Learner Cohort Bounds}: Phase 1 evaluation relies on computational agent profiles parameterized by Markov transition models \cite{corbett1994knowledge, yudelson2013individualized}. Live human trials are subject to individual cognitive variance and emotional factors.
    \item \textbf{Subject Domain Scope}: Current submodules focus on computer science topics (OS, DS, DBMS, CN). Generalizability across non-technical domains requires broader empirical testing.
    \item \textbf{Interaction Horizon}: Simulation trials span 6 sequential sessions per user. Longitudinal retention tracking over months remains for future clinical study.
    \item \textbf{Potential Novelty Effect}: Interactive video checkpoints may induce initial engagement boosts that require long-term tracking to isolate from core cognitive gain.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{BKT vs. Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT)}
BKT functions deterministically from initial attempts without requiring thousands of pre-training interactions needed by neural networks \cite{piech2015deep}. BKT parameters map directly to interpretable cognitive states.
